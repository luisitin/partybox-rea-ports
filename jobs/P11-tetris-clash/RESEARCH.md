## Rotation and pieces

The [public SRS summary](https://tetris.wiki/Super_Rotation_System) agrees with the paper and independently inspected implementations: four orientations; shared kick offsets for J/L/S/T/Z; separate I offsets; O has no visible turn. Canonical offsets use positive y upward. Our board coordinates increase downward, so negate the vertical offset when checking a candidate. Test both rotation directions, floor kicks, I wall kicks, blocked turns, and four turns returning to the same cells.

The [public random-generator summary](https://tetris.wiki/Random_Generator) agrees with the two bag implementations. Use the SDK's seeded shuffle, not a comparator-based random sort or random draws with replacement. Block Clash generates each bag by its deterministic index from the match's piece seed and stores each seat's cursor; it does not store a growing sequence. Hold may advance a player's cursor without affecting anyone else's order. Show a small preview, but do not serialize the seed, RNG state, future sequence cursor, or another seat's private hold/next data into views.

## Scoring and attacks

The licensed Xbox help rewards difficult clears, consecutive clears and back-to-back difficult clears. The inspected Kotlin implementation uses normal attack counts 0/1/2/4 for one/two/three/four rows, and T-spin attacks 2/4/6. Its garbage packets contain a single hole column. Its exact combo and spin detection are implementation choices, not proof that every licensed release uses identical rules. The [garbage summary](https://tetris.wiki/Garbage) also documents differences between games.

Block Clash should document its complete score and attack tables in its game README rather than claiming certification. Keep the normal attack table recognizable; any simplification of mini-spins, perfect clears, combo growth or lock timing is an explicit port decision. Resolve cancellation before queuing the excess for the selected living opponent. Check ceiling overflow before discarding lifted rows. A target who is eliminated or leaves must be replaced deterministically.

## Round structure and living-room adaptation

The original battle loop is continuous play until one player remains; licensed timed variants compare performance against a clock. P11 explicitly chooses two minutes or last standing. PartyBox's shell already owns ready-up and the countdown. Enter live play immediately, show a short score reveal, then final results. No second rules countdown is needed.

The phone is the precise play surface. Keep its board, ghost landing, next piece, pending garbage, target name and thumb controls visible together at 390 by 844. Tap rotates, horizontal swipes move, and a deliberate downward gesture drops; retain large labeled buttons so a player does not have to discover gestures. Selection of a rival must not require a scrolling carousel during play. Preview the drop destination before committing it. A short hint above the controls can disappear after the first successful input.

The TV is the shared drama: every board, large player names, survival state, score and pending attacks. At eight seats, a four by two arrangement offers comparison without asking people to steer from the TV. An attack needs a named source/destination or line-count announcement, not only a color flash. Keep eliminations visible and animate them briefly while surviving players continue. Phone-only players receive their own full board plus compact standings; eliminated players and spectators receive the stage without controls or instructions to look elsewhere.

Reconnect resumes the authoritative board and target. A disconnected seat's gravity keeps advancing, so it cannot block play or evade garbage. Pausing shifts every gameplay clock, not only the phase deadline. Bots should evaluate reachable placements for holes, height, roughness and cleared rows, then issue ordinary move/rotate/drop inputs. Difficulty changes strategic quality and hesitation; it does not grant different pieces or extra garbage.

## Feel, sound and verification priorities

The intended pleasure is a readable landing, a planned multi-line clear, a visible threat to a named rival, and a last-second defense through cancellation. These are design inferences from the documented mechanics; live timings and original sound cues were not measured. Use original visual feedback for placement, row clears and attacks. If audio is added, it must be generated/original and optional; never reproduce the licensed theme.

Prioritize tests for simultaneous deadlines, stale timers, late inputs, pause/resume, garbage overflow, attack cancellation, unknown IDs, target loss, same-seed replay, and seven-bag fairness. Simulation must cover 2/4/8 seats and all bot strengths. Capture every phase, phone-only and spectator states. Verification results are recorded separately and must not be inferred from this research.
